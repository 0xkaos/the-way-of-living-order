## What we are comparing

The framework connects several questions that other traditions sometimes treat separately:

- **Reality:** living beings are ongoing organized activity with a history, rather than merely static arrangements.
- **Value:** there is a real standard grounded in the design and trajectory that produced us. Pleasure can accompany fulfillment without defining it.
- **Obligation:** choosing actions does not confer authority to choose whether that standard applies. Value above a minimum need not all become duty.
- **Knowledge of the standard:** the proposed reference is life's history before the emergence of the relevant capacity to model and deliberately better one's own form. This is a window, not a frozen ancestral state.
- **Religion:** traditions attempt to convey the underlying truth with varying fidelity. The knowable God of humanity is distinguished from an unknowable ultimate origin.

These are the author's proposals, with arguments and definitions still developing. In particular, recognizing biological functions and establishing moral obligations are distinguishable achievements. None of the comparisons below resolves that connection simply by supplying an established name for it.

## 1. Whitehead and process theology

**Shared ground.** Whitehead makes becoming, relation, and experience fundamental. His actual entities are events of experience, not necessarily conscious minds. The resemblance to your account concerns understanding living form through its activity and relations. You have not thereby committed to his account of the ultimate constituents of all reality. See *Process and Reality*, Part I, chapter II, §I, printed p. 18 ([primary text](https://palmyreoomen.nl/uploads/pdf%27s/A.N.Whitehead_Process-and-Reality.pdf)).

**Value is already present.** Whitehead's process theology is not a neutral description of change to which purpose has never been added. In John Cobb's exposition, God offers possibilities for realization and responds to the world's experience. That is a substantive metaphysical claim, extending beyond treating religious language as a metaphor for biological history. [Cobb, “Process Theology”](https://www.religion-online.org/article/process-theology/), an account by a participant in the tradition.

**The sharpest theological difference.** Whitehead gives God a primordial nature concerned with possibilities and a consequent nature receiving the world. These are aspects of one God, not your two referents. He distinguishes the respects in which permanence and change apply; the combination therefore is not, by itself, a contradiction. His primordial nature is also not simply an event before spacetime. See *Process and Reality*, Part V, chapter II, §§III–V, printed pp. 345–349, especially 345 and 348 ([PDF, beginning at page 366](https://palmyreoomen.nl/uploads/pdf%27s/A.N.Whitehead_Process-and-Reality.pdf#page=366)).

**Your possible departure.** Your proposal asks life's development before reflective self-modification to disclose an authoritative direction. That reference window is a more specific claim than the general importance of becoming. It may also let you develop the book without adopting Whitehead's entire cosmology.

**For discussion:** Which Whiteheadian commitments do you actually need? Is the incompleteness primarily an insufficiently explicit human standard, the account of God, or the difficulty of translating the philosophy into a compelling public narrative? These possibilities should not silently become one criticism.

## 2. Naturalism: several different comparisons

Naturalism is a family of positions. It does not, by definition, mean that morality is imaginary. The distinction between methodological and ontological naturalism is useful here; boundaries and formulations vary. [David Papineau, “Naturalism,” Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/naturalism/), scholarly overview, introduction consulted.

| Sense | Meaning for this comparison | Provisional relation to your framework |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Methodological | Investigate through experience and inquiry continuous with the sciences. | Strong resemblance in your demand for an explicit natural foundation. This need not make philosophy reducible to laboratory science. |
| Ontological | Reality contains only what belongs to nature. | Not yet your stated position: you distinguish an unknowable origin rather than clearly ruling out everything beyond nature. |
| Ethical | Moral truth is grounded in natural facts or features of beings. | A promising classification for the proposed basis of value and obligation; Foot supplies a concrete example below. |
| Religious | Natural reality can sustain religious meaning and orientation. | A useful comparison for your treatment of God and inherited narratives; Wieman is a particularly relevant example below. |

Your distinctive task within this family is to specify **which natural facts have authority and why**. “Natural” alone cannot discriminate between repair, disease, cooperation, and exploitation. Your argument already seeks a more discriminating direction; the word naturalism should not replace that argument.

A provisional description is **a naturalistic account of objective value and purpose, with a religious interpretation**. This is an editorial aid, not a proposed final name or a commitment to physicalism.

## 3. Kantian ethics

**An important agreement.** Kantian autonomy does not mean inventing whatever morality one prefers. The rational will gives itself universal law; desire and convenience do not determine duty. This makes Kant an ally against the idea that choosing conduct makes obligation optional. [Kant, *Groundwork*, Section II, discussion of autonomy and heteronomy](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm).

**The foundational disagreement.** Kant locates the basis of moral necessity in reason, not the empirical constitution or history of humanity. Your proposal instead gives our inherited design and its direction that authority. For Kant, an appeal to a desired result cannot establish categorical duty. See the same work's Preface and Section II.

Your reply is not adequately represented by “I happen to want survival, so I should pursue it.” You say the relevant end is authoritative whether or not an individual accepts it. The disagreement concerns what makes that authority valid, not merely whether moral requirements can be declined.

Kant also treats humanity as an end in itself. Therefore “Kant has no place for ends” would misdescribe him; the issue is the source and status of the end. Your minimum-obligation distinction must remain visible in any comparison with duty.

**For discussion:** What does your natural-history account establish that a rational-agency account cannot? Conversely, what prevents inherited function from being used to subordinate a person to collective capacity? The older proposals about care and constituent agency may help, but require your renewed endorsement and development.

## 4. Aristotle and Philippa Foot: natural goodness

This is a particularly close ethical comparison. Aristotle connects the human good with excellent rational activity across a life. The useful link is between what a being is and what constitutes living well, rather than treating pleasure as a sufficient standard. [*Nicomachean Ethics* I.7](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html), primary passage consulted.

Foot develops evaluation through the characteristic life of a kind of organism, including human practical reason. In her later position, someone's indifference to a reason does not make the reason disappear. Her account is therefore especially relevant to your rejection of freedom over the ought. [Foot's 2001 interview](https://philosophynow.org/issues/41/Philippa_Foot), primary account of her views; *Natural Goodness* itself has not been closely reviewed here.

The difference to investigate is **life-form versus historical trajectory**. Your proposed standard draws on a direction across the history of life, with a particular boundary at reflective self-modification. Foot's account does not require that reference window. Her approach also brings rational and social human life into the account, rather than identifying goodness with sheer organism survival.

**For discussion:** Does the framework judge human excellence by what humans characteristically need, by life's wider direction, or by a specified relation between the two? What follows when those descriptions appear to conflict?

## 5. Aquinas and natural law

Aquinas describes natural inclinations concerning preservation, offspring, and rational social life as relevant to practical reason. Natural law participates in eternal law; its authority is not created by an individual's preference. [*Summa Theologiae* I–II, q. 94, a. 2](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2094.htm), and [q. 91, a. 2](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2091.htm), primary articles consulted.

This is close to your account of discoverable requirements within our constitution and to your “written in our hearts” language. Natural-law reasoning is not equivalent to biblical literalism.

Your historical and evolutionary route, and your separation of the two God referents, are important departures from Aquinas's account. Similarity in natural obligation does not establish agreement about creation, divine unity, or particular moral rules.

**For discussion:** Which part of your claim could be presented as revising natural law in light of natural history, and which part would require replacing its foundation?

## 6. Henry Nelson Wieman: naturalistic religious thought

Wieman asks what reality deserves religious commitment because it creates human life and its good. He distinguishes that question from simply cataloguing what people actually worship. This is an unusually close starting question to yours. [“The Confessions of a Religious Seeker”](https://www.harvardsquarelibrary.org/theology-philosophy/appendix-henry-nelson-wieman-the-confessions-of-a-religious-seeker/), primary autobiographical essay, opening and discussion of creativity consulted.

Cobb describes Wieman's account in terms of creative interchange in human affairs. [“Process Theology”](https://www.religion-online.org/article/process-theology/), opening discussion of naturalistic process thought.

Your proposed reference extends much further back, across life before reflective human self-modification. That creates a potentially important disagreement: later human creativity can contribute to the path, but cannot simply certify its own products as the standard.

**For discussion:** What makes a creative process good rather than merely productive? This question also connects to your concern about institutional mission drift. Wieman deserves a closer reading before any claim that the two frameworks coincide.

## 7. Spinoza: persistence, nature, and God

Spinoza's conatus concerns a thing's striving to persevere in its being. Humans belong within nature, and God is not a humanlike external ruler. However, his God concerns reality as a whole, and he rejects final causes attributed to nature. [*Ethics*, Part I, propositions 14–15 and Appendix; Part III, propositions 6–7](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3800/pg3800-images.html), selected primary passages consulted.

The resemblance is strongest around persistence and a natural understanding of humanity. Your knowable God of humanity is more specifically tied to the history producing living and human form. Your distinction from an unknowable origin also does not map straightforwardly onto Spinoza's single substance.

**For discussion:** Can your directional purpose be stated entirely through living organization and its requirements, or does it assert something further about why nature produces that organization? A direction without a final endpoint need not imply a predetermined cosmic destination.

## 8. Hans Jonas: life and responsibility

Jonas's writing on technology connects expanded human power with enlarged responsibility for consequences. [“Toward a Philosophy of Technology”](https://catalogimages.wiley.com/images/db/pdf/9781405149013.excerpt.pdf), primary essay, selected passages on responsibility consulted. The biological philosophy of *The Phenomenon of Life* is a promising further comparison, but only its [publisher description](https://nupress.northwestern.edu/9780810117495/the-phenomenon-of-life/) has been reviewed here.

For your framework, Jonas raises a practical issue: greater capacity can generate greater danger as well as opportunity. What restrains an apparent advance when it threatens the living conditions on which future agency depends?

Treat the claim that Jonas is a close biological-metaphysical neighbor as a research lead, not the result of a completed comparison of his books.

## 9. Elizabeth Anscombe: inherited moral vocabulary

Anscombe argues that a special lawlike sense of moral obligation can survive after belief in its divine lawgiver has been abandoned. She questions retaining that sense without its supporting conception. [“Modern Moral Philosophy” (1958)](https://www.freddoso.com/papers/AnscombeMMP.pdf), opening thesis and discussion of law conceptions; the scan's PDF page 3 contains printed pp. 30–31 of this reprint.

This closely resembles your concern about unexamined inherited “should” language. Your proposed response is to articulate an authoritative natural foundation. The resemblance does not establish your narrower empirical claim about contemporary American usage, and Anscombe's historical diagnosis should itself remain distinguishable from that claim.

**For discussion:** Does explaining the end implicit in “should” supply everything obligation requires, or is there an additional account of authority to give? Your rejection of ambiguity is intended to address that second issue.

## 10. Christine Korsgaard: constitutivism and self-formation

Constitutivism seeks standards arising from what it is to be an agent. Korsgaard connects those standards with an agent's constitution of herself through action. [“Constitutivism and the Virtues” (2019)](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/4eb5c0ef-ca39-4635-b570-dc2b5339a373), author's abstract consulted; the full article and *Self-Constitution* remain for closer reading.

This is relevant to modeling and bettering one's own form. The crucial comparison is between standards constitutive of rational agency and standards disclosed by the natural history that produced agency. Neither position should be paraphrased as choosing one's obligations arbitrarily.

**For discussion:** Does reflective choice reveal an already binding standard, introduce additional requirements specific to agency, or both?

## 11. Biological function and organizational normativity

Mossio, Saborido, and Moreno develop an account of biological functions grounded in the current organization of self-maintaining systems. They argue that this can explain non-arbitrary standards of functional success and failure. [“An Organizational Account of Biological Functions” (2009)](https://www.ias-research.net/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Mossio_25592036.pdf), abstract and introduction consulted; downloaded text was readable when the browser extraction failed.

This is a direct research connection for your description of life: organization supplies conditions under which something can function well or badly. Its emphasis on current organization also provides a contrast with your historical reference window.

The article's argument concerns biological functions. It does not, by itself, establish a single scale of human moral value, settle conflicts between organisms, or prove the obligation to continue life's direction. It can help make one part of your foundation more precise without being represented as proof of the whole framework.

## 12. John Dewey and pragmatism

Dewey describes ends-in-view as anticipated consequences arising within activity and guiding its continuation. [*Human Nature and Conduct*, Part III, discussion of ends](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41386/41386-h/41386-h.htm), selected primary passage consulted.

This connects with the older paper's interest in learning, correction, and institutions. Those remain candidates for renewed commentary. The disagreement to investigate concerns the authority of the governing direction: your framework aims to identify a standard that later choices must answer to, while Dewey's account helps examine how particular ends emerge and are revised in practice.

Do not turn this into “fixed destination versus change”: you also reject a fixed final destination. Distinguish revising a plan, revising our knowledge of the good, and revising what makes something good.

Your proposed **historical reference window**, the **distinction between two God referents**, and the **connection from design to nonoptional obligation** deserve particularly exact comparisons. Their combination may help identify the book's contribution; no claim of uniqueness is established by this first pass.

For manuscript drafting, explain your own claim first, then use a nearby thinker to expose the precise agreement or disagreement. A general reader should not need to complete a history-of-philosophy course to understand the argument.
