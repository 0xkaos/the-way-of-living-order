Life is matter organized into self-maintaining, self-repairing, reproducing form. That organization arose as dissipative structuring under energy flux and was sharpened by selection against the most resistant elements. Its history traces a direction — not toward any final destination, but a distinct and discernible trajectory: the maintenance, repair, and reproduction of organization, extended and amplified from the first cell through bodies, language, and social organization. The window from the onset of life to the emergence of choice is where that trajectory can be read with confidence; after choice emerges, outcomes may reflect bad choices and mislead us as standards.

I call this trajectory the path. What religion calls God, I understand as its personification. God, the path, and the trajectory are one and the same — the knowable God of humanity, distinct from the unknowable origin that precedes spacetime and causation, which I do not claim to address.

Value is real: a thing's value is its potential to support the maintenance, repair, and reproduction of organization, and the value realized depends on application. A society's values align with the path where they serve this, and societies can also hold values that depart from it; social approval is not the standard.

Obligation is a covenant. The path does not command; it offers terms. Those terms were set and kept across the whole history of life before any creature could ask why — every ancestor back to the first cell held them, and our existence is the lineage's kept covenant. The offer renews personally with the emergence of choice, the only capacity that can breach it knowingly. We are free to breach; we are not free to renegotiate the terms or to exit the world in which their consequences unfold. There is no further "must" behind the covenant, and none is needed: the demand for one is the confusion left over when the lawgiver was forgotten. The promise: accordance will be rewarding. It is written in our hearts; satisfaction is not the rule or the guide, but the promise. And the promise is a strong tendency, not a guarantee — tragedy can erase good without breach, and that loss, though real and permanent, proves nothing.

The sanction: departures from the path tend toward poor maintenance, repair, and reproduction of the organization that practices them, or act as cancer on the larger organization on which they depend. This is the voice of God — how to live, and what goes wrong when we do not listen.

Duty is a minimum threshold; value beyond it is possible and good but not obligatory. Individuals bear the greater obligation to enact the path; institutions that maintain and promote social order bear the greater obligation to know it.

A soul is an individual's totality of existence and impact. The afterlife is that life's lasting impact — progeny, good memories, lasting productivity. To leave no enduring good is damnation, though what the covenant promises is tendency, not certainty, and absence alone is not a verdict.

All religions are attempts to convey this underlying truth, some more proximally than others, and all capable of disastrous mission drift. Literalism *can mistake(s) the personification for an external party; metaphorical understandings can carry the truth. This book articulates the foundation.
