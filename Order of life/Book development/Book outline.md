# Provisional book outline

Status: one possible reader journey, proposed by the assistant. Chapter titles, order, audience, and commitments remain open.

| Chapter | Reader's question | Job of the chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The question that started this | Why should I care about this problem? | Begin with an experience or concrete dilemma; establish the aim of articulating real value and purpose underlying human behavior and social systems. Make the foundational scope explicit. |
| 2. What a living thing maintains | What distinguishes the organization this account values? | Explain the relevant observations and define terms through examples. Mark scientific uncertainties. |
| 3. The history we inherit | How is a trajectory discerned from the history of life? | Clarify constraint, contingency, function, and the proposed historical window before the emergence of choice. Explain how its direction is identified. Consider Michaelian's pigment-world hypothesis as a possible extension to life's genesis itself, with its evidential status distinct from the later history. |
| 4. The direction in our values | How do familiar value judgments reflect a direction older than human choice, and how can socially endorsed values depart from it? | Explain the proposed relationship between existing values and the archaic path. Use the criterion of potential for maintenance, repair, and reproduction of organization to examine both alignment and immoral social values. State and defend the foundation without making a new catalogue of rankings the chapter's main task. Develop independent durability across organizational shells: a group's dependence on a larger organization extends evaluation to that organization's needs. Address conflicts between systems and distinguish local benefit from damage to larger organization. |
| 5. When choice becomes obligation | Why should this bind me, and how much does it demand? | Distinguish ordinary choice from the proposed capacity to model and deliberately better one's own form, leaving determinism open. Explain why later outcomes can be misleading standards. Present the normative argument using the earlier trajectory. Distinguish value from minimum duty and individuals' responsibility to enact from institutions' greater responsibility to know. |
| 6. Why use the word God? | How does the knowable source of human obligation relate to an unknowable ultimate origin? | Explain the author's distinction, its scope, and the objection to unifying the referents. Keep philosophical interpretation distinct from the history of divine names. |
| 7. Narrative and religious inheritance | How can religions convey a common truth with differing fidelity and the risk of mission drift? | Explain the author's account of religions as attempts to convey the underlying truth. Develop the author's proposed meanings of soul and afterlife as focused examples; examine the judgment about leaving no enduring good. Distinguish literalism from truth conveyed metaphorically, and examine the possibility of drift in every tradition. Leave detailed textual and practical extrapolation to readers and specialists; check historical claims actually used. |
| 8. Comparisons and intellectual context | What does this framework build on, revise, or dismiss? | Use [[Philosophical comparisons]] and [[Michaelian and the origin of form]] to acknowledge related work and state selected agreements and disagreements. The author freely chooses the framework's commitments; resemblance does not imply allegiance to an entire tradition. |
| 9. What follows, and what remains open | How would accepting this change judgment or conduct? | Work through difficult examples, state the framework's limits, and identify unresolved questions. |

If religion is the reader's main point of entry, the question in chapter 6 may need to appear much earlier. If the author begins from a personal experience, it may supply the thread across chapters. Decide after the first conversation and sample essay.

The comparisons chapter is author-requested. Its position as chapter 8 remains an assistant proposal; the framework should be intelligible before a reader is asked to assess its relation to other systems.

Scope clarified by the author: the book develops a philosophical foundation for understanding human behavior, social systems, value, and purpose. Founding a new religion or constructing a comprehensive religious, moral, and practical system is outside scope. Detailed biblical interpretation is not a required manuscript task, and the chapter on narrative should not become a story-by-story commentary.

The author emphasizes explaining the prior direction reflected in existing values, including the possibility of immoral values within a society. Object rankings and scientific superlatives are subsidiary examples; resolving every ranking is not a prerequisite to developing the central argument.

## A chapter needs

- A question the reader can understand before learning new terminology.
- A main claim and the reasoning that supports it.
- A concrete example that does real explanatory work.
- An objection capable of changing the argument.
- A clear account of what has been established and what the next chapter still owes the reader.

## First writing experiment

Draft a short standalone essay around one case from the author's experience. Explain how the framework changes its interpretation, then let a skeptical reader challenge the explanation. Use this to discover missing premises and a suitable voice before committing to a full chapter sequence.
