# Voice and revision

The book's voice is not yet chosen. The starting proposal is precise, accessible prose that allows uncertainty and makes abstract ideas tangible.

## Audience

The author wants to reach curious general readers and people interested in religion and meaning, including atheists. Conceptual difficulty is expected.

Editorial proposal: give readers enough explanation to follow difficult ideas and enough explicit reasoning to assess them from different starting beliefs. Introduce the intended meaning of “God” carefully; distinguish understanding the proposal from agreeing with it. This proposal remains open to the author's revision.

## Working principles

- Begin a difficult idea with something the reader can picture.
- Introduce terminology when it solves a problem, then use it consistently.
- Give each paragraph a clear purpose: observation, explanation, argument, example, or objection.
- Match confidence to support. An interpretation, hypothesis, and demonstrated result should sound different.
- Let objections receive their strongest formulation. Answer them without treating disagreement as a failure of intelligence or character.
- Use religious and scientific vocabulary with care; explain exactly what a term contributes.
- Preserve the author's characteristic expressions when they are clear. Polish should not erase the person thinking.

## Three possible voices

These are assistant-written style samples, not approved manuscript passages or established conclusions.

**Conversational**

> A living thing has to keep going. It takes in what it needs and repairs what it can. My question begins there: can the conditions of staying alive tell us anything about how to live?

**Reflective**

> We inherit a body before we can ask what it is for. Hunger, injury, and dependence make their claims on us long before we have a philosophy. I want to ask what, if anything, those conditions teach us about obligation.

**Analytical**

> The proposal connects the conditions of viable living organization with reasons for action. Its central task is to explain why those conditions should bind an agent, including one who does not endorse continued organization as an end.

React to these by identifying sentences you would and would not say. We can build the style from your reactions and from a short sample of your own unedited speech or writing.

## Revision passes

| Pass | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| Meaning | Does this accurately express the author's position? What did the assistant add? |
| Argument | Are the premises explicit, the inferences defensible, and the objections answered? |
| Structure | Does the reader receive explanations in the order they need them? |
| Evidence | Do the scientific, historical, and textual claims have adequate sources? |
| Prose | Can each sentence become clearer, more concrete, or more natural? |
| Copy edit | Are terminology, spelling, references, and formatting consistent? |

Work primarily on one pass at a time. Major changes in meaning return a passage to argument review even if its sentences were already polished.

## Useful requests to the assistant

- “Interview me about this idea, one question at a time. Restate my answer before proposing an extension.”
- “Separate what I explicitly claim from what you infer.”
- “Give the strongest objection to this paragraph and identify the premise under dispute.”
- “Show two revisions: one preserving my wording closely, and one reorganizing it. Explain any change in meaning.”
- “Draft from these approved claims. Mark missing support rather than inventing it.”

For a substantial edit, retain the old passage and record the reason for changes in meaning. Wording changes can be grouped; new philosophical commitments deserve individual attention.
